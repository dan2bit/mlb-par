What is PAR?

Baseball's payroll gap is enormous — the Dodgers spend about eleven times what the Nationals do. PAR asks one question: which clubs are beating what their spending implies, and which are falling short of it?

It's a valuation, not a forecast. Think of payroll as the market's price on a club; PAR scores each result against that price.

How a game is scored

Every game is still one win and one loss. What changes is how much they count.

  • When the higher-paid club wins, the market got what it paid for. The game counts 1–1, like any ordinary game.
  • When the lower-paid club wins, it's an upset. The underdog is credited more than one win and the favourite is debited exactly the same in losses — the bigger the payroll gap, the bigger the credit, up to 1.44.

Nothing is invented: every credit to one club is an identical debit to the other.

The 28–72% guard

Payroll alone would price the Dodgers at 77% to beat the Nationals in a single game. No sensible market posts a baseball game at 77% — even the best team loses about a third of its games, and the worst wins about a quarter. So no club is ever priced outside 28–72% for one game, which is what caps the largest upset at 1.44.

Why PAR shows more games than were played

Upsets add weight; favoured wins never subtract any. So the adjusted totals come to more than the real schedule. That's deliberate — the alternative, docking a favourite for winning, punished good expensive clubs for doing their job. Compare clubs on PAR% rather than raw adjusted W-L; the ratio is the meaningful part.

The tails

The most expensive club can never record an upset win, because no club is richer. The cheapest can never record a favoured win. Those zeros are facts about the league's payroll structure, not quirks in the arithmetic.
